\begin{abstract}
We present a reproducible framework for Chennai, India, that combines relative terrain susceptibility (30\,m, seven DEM-derived indicators), Sentinel-1 backscatter diagnostics for eight 2021 acquisitions, exposure and partial vulnerability indicators, and administrative and crowd-sourced flood reports. Every input carries an explicit evidence class. The only independent flood references obtainable were reported locations: 561 (2021) and 306 (2015) inundated locations listed by the Greater Chennai Corporation, and 2{,}350 crowd-flagged street segments (2 December 2015; 2{,}307 inside the region). They give the monsoon year only. Re-reading the source document exposed column-shift errors in a public extraction. The two independent 2015 sources agree spatially 2.5 times more often than a street-network null model. 2021 reports show no association with bright-backscatter radar components at 0--100\,m. Class-1 radar water-change candidates are small (0.2--1.8\,km$^2$), change between dates (median Jaccard 0.11) and do not follow rainfall. Treating reported wards as presences and all other wards as unlabelled, we evaluated whether any indicator set ranks reported wards above the background. We used zone-blocked, buffered cross-validation, within-zone AUC, global and zone-stratified permutation nulls, and multiplicity correction over 16 tests. No indicator set survives correction. A weak within-zone signal in 2015 (AUC about 0.6) is not significant after correction, and nothing appears in 2021. Reporting-based labels in this setting encode reporting opportunity rather than a learnable physical signal. We therefore release exploratory structure (two stable ward clusters, Moran's $I$ up to 0.77) and a data contract specifying what supervised flood prediction would require, instead of a prediction map.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Chennai has flooded repeatedly, most severely in November--December 2015 and again during the 2021 northeast monsoon. Satellite and modelling studies of the 2015 event map large inundated areas \cite{ramasamy2018chennai,chundeli2018chennai,iwmi2015chennai}. Machine-learning studies often build flood ``susceptibility'' or ``prediction'' maps from such inventories. When the labels are uncertain, temporally coarse or produced by the same sensor used as a predictor, the resulting accuracy figures can describe the labels more than the floods.

This paper asks a narrower question: \emph{given the flood evidence that can actually be obtained and verified for Chennai, what can a multi-source geospatial framework legitimately establish?} We contribute:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an integrated, reproducible pipeline that keeps susceptibility, exposure, vulnerability and flood evidence as separate, hash-verified layers with explicit evidence classes (E1 mapped extent to E5 context);
\item a source-level verification of an official ward-level inundation list, including correction of a published extraction;
\item a multi-date description of Sentinel-1 water-change candidates against terrain susceptibility and rainfall;
\item a presence-background evaluation with spatial blocks, buffers, within-zone AUC, global and zone-stratified permutation nulls, multiplicity correction and an area control, showing that the available labels do not support a learned flood model;
\item an open dashboard and data contract that carry these limits into use.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Terrain indices such as the topographic wetness index (TWI) \cite{beven1979twi} and height above nearest drainage (HAND) \cite{nobre2011hand} are standard building blocks of susceptibility models. Sentinel-1 C-band SAR \cite{torres2012s1} is widely used for flood mapping. In cities, however, open water appears dark only where it is visible to the radar. Flooded streets can instead brighten through double bounce, and layover and shadow hide much of the ground \cite{mason2018urban,chini2019coherence,zhao2024review}. Risk frameworks separate hazard, exposure and vulnerability \cite{ipcc2012srex}. Composite social vulnerability indices \cite{cutter2003sovi} require demographic inputs that are often unavailable at ward level in Indian cities. Presence-only data are routine in species-distribution modelling, where presence-background discrimination \cite{phillips2006maxent} and positive-unlabelled learning \cite{elkan2008pu} replace accuracy against true negatives. Spatially structured data require blocked cross-validation \cite{roberts2017cv,valavi2019blockcv}. We carry these practices into urban flood assessment.

\section{Study Area and Data}
The analysis grid is EPSG:32644 at 30\,m ($1416\times726$ cells). It covers a provisional, OpenStreetMap-derived study area of about 435\,km$^2$ (483{,}727 cells), which is \emph{not} the official corporation boundary. Analysis units are the 200 wards of the Greater Chennai Corporation (GCC) from the state GIS (TNGIS, 432\,km$^2$). Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the inputs and their evidence classes. Rainfall from CHIRPS \cite{funk2015chirps} is context only: about 29 native 0.05$^\circ$ cells intersect the study area, and the 200 wards take only 22 distinct CHIRPS event totals, so it carries no ward-scale information. Sentinel-1A IW GRD scenes (descending, relative orbit 92, about 06:01 IST) were processed in Google Earth Engine \cite{gorelick2017gee} for eight dates between 5 September and 28 November 2021.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Relative susceptibility (SUS-1)}
Seven indicators (elevation, slope, TWI, log flow accumulation, distance to drainage, a Euclidean HAND proxy, fill depth) are rank- or robust-min--max scored within the scored domain and averaged with equal weights. The three wetness-family indicators (slope, TWI, flow accumulation) hold 43\% of the nominal weight but explain 52\% of the index variance (TWI alone 21\%), so the index partly double-counts wetness. Water-signature and missing cells (12{,}255) are \emph{not assessed} and never scored as low. A six-indicator variant without TWI agrees closely (Spearman 0.982; top-decile Jaccard 0.890) and is kept as a sensitivity case only. This shows robustness to one construction choice, not validity. The index is relative, uncalibrated and not a probability.

\subsection{Flood evidence and its verification}
The GCC City Disaster Management Plan prints, per ward, the locations inundated in each monsoon year with a depth class (${<}2$, 2--3, 3--5, ${>}5$\,ft). A public table extraction was re-read against the PDF: for every page, the rotated column headers were recovered from the character stream and each printed mark was assigned to its nearest header, row by row. This verified 1{,}312 of 1{,}401 rows and exposed column shifts in the extraction: the 3--5\,ft class was lost in 72 rows and relabelled in 78 more. Locations were placed by a gazetteer within their printed ward. Each received a positional uncertainty of 50--750\,m by match type, and unplaced records were kept, not dropped. The project's reference checker classified the 2021 list as \textsc{presence\_only} and both 2015 sets as \textsc{qualitative\_only} with respect to the 2021 radar dates.

\subsection{Null-model comparisons}
For two sets of reports, or reports versus radar components, we report the share of locations within $d$ of the other set, divided by the same share for random points on the street network (representative points of 34{,}355 road polygons). We use 200 draws.

\subsection{Multi-date radar context}
For the eight dates we summarise class-1 ``transient dark'' candidates of the accepted water-change product (E4): area per date, date-to-date overlap (Jaccard), persistence, concentration in wards (top-10 share, Gini), and enrichment across SUS-1 deciles (share of candidate cells in a decile divided by the decile's share of scored cells). CHIRPS antecedent rainfall is set beside them (Spearman over $n=8$ dates, descriptive). All inputs are hash-guarded and unchanged.

\subsection{Evidence-aware ward analytics}
For 198 wards with complete data, 14 indicators are log-transformed where skewed and standardised. They cover SUS-1, terrain, Sentinel-1, exposure and vulnerability. Report-derived quantities, coarse rainfall and partial-coverage population are excluded. We report:
\begin{enumerate}
\item principal components;
\item $k$-means clusters selected by silhouette \cite{rousseeuw1987silhouette} subject to bootstrap stability (adjusted Rand index \cite{hubert1985ari} $\ge 0.75$, 100 resamples);
\item Moran's $I$ \cite{moran1950} with contiguity weights;
\item a presence-background analysis.
\end{enumerate}
In the presence-background analysis, wards with $\geq 1$ listed location in an event year are presences. All others are \emph{background}, explicitly not negatives. Logistic regression and random forests \cite{breiman2001rf,pedregosa2011sklearn} are evaluated by out-of-fold presence-background AUC (PB-AUC) under leave-one-zone-out cross-validation (15 zones). Training wards within 500\,m of the test zone are removed. Each PB-AUC is compared with a label-permutation null that re-runs the full cross-validation. Controls are a ward-area-only model, a population-density-only model, and cross-event transfer (2015$\to$2021 and back). Pooled out-of-fold AUC ranks scores from 15 separately trained zone models together; those scores share no common scale and zone prevalence differs, so pooled AUC mixes within-zone ranking with between-zone offsets. We therefore also report the \emph{within-zone} PB-AUC, computed only over presence--background pairs in the same test zone, and test it against a \emph{zone-stratified} null that shuffles labels within zones and so preserves each zone's prevalence (200 permutations for logistic regression, 40 for random forests). Sixteen feature-set, model and event combinations are tested; $p$-values are adjusted with Holm's method and the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Reported evidence}
The 2015 sources are mutually consistent (Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}, Table~\ref{tab:cmp}). GCC locations lie within 50\,m of a crowd-flagged street 2.51 times as often as street-null points do (0 of 200 null draws reached the observed share). The reverse direction gives 2.27. In 2021, the GCC locations are no closer to bright-VV components than random street points at 0 and 100\,m (ratios 0.37--1.09). The only small excess occurs at 250\,m on two dates (97--98\% of reports vs.\ null means of 93--95\%). Ninety-six wards are listed in both years, but only seven exact location names recur.

\subsection{Radar candidates across eight dates}
Class-1 candidates are small and unstable (Fig.~\ref{fig:s1tc}): 0.21--1.85\,km$^2$ per date (${<}0.5\%$ of the study area). The median overlap between two dates is 0.11 (Jaccard), and 53\% of the 4.2\,km$^2$ ever flagged were flagged on one date only. They are concentrated: ten wards hold 74--95\% of each date's candidate area. They sit high in SUS-1 (mean percentile 81--86; 3.4 times over-represented in the top decile, median over dates). This is internal consistency, not validation, because both products use DEM slope. Candidate area does not follow antecedent rainfall ($|\rho|\le0.31$, $n=8$). On 28 November, after about 250\,mm in the prior seven days, only 1.2\,km$^2$ were flagged. Dark-water detection does not capture flooded streets in the built-up city \cite{mason2018urban}.

\subsection{Ward structure}
PC1 explains 46\% of the indicator variance. It contrasts low-lying, flat, drainage-proximal wards with high SUS-1 percentiles against the dense core (population, roads, facilities). Two clusters are moderately stable: silhouette 0.30 (weak separation), mean bootstrap ARI 0.85 but a 5th percentile of 0.54, and agreement with Ward linkage 0.86. Larger $k$ is less stable (mean $\mathrm{ARI}\le0.79$). The clusters are indicator-profile groups, not flood-severity classes. The 54 peripheral wards of cluster~0 are physically most susceptible but least dense (Fig.~\ref{fig:clusters}). All key variables are spatially autocorrelated: Moran's $I$ is 0.77 for SUS-1, 0.39 for density and 0.10--0.24 for report counts, all with permutation $p<0.02$.

\subsection{Presence-background analysis}
Prevalence is high: 119 of 198 wards are presences in 2015 and 147 of 198 in 2021. Pooled PB-AUC for the 12 indicator models ranges from 0.39 to 0.54, inside its global permutation null ($p\ge0.19$). Within zones the picture differs by year (Fig.~\ref{fig:pb}). In 2021 no model ranks presences above background (within-zone AUC 0.39--0.57, zone-stratified $p\ge0.11$). In 2015, within-zone AUCs are 0.50--0.62. The physical-indicator logistic model (0.62) and the all-indicator forest (0.61) are nominally above the stratified null ($p=0.030$ and $0.024$), but neither survives correction for 16 tests (Holm $p\ge0.39$; BH $q\ge0.24$). The ward-area control reaches 0.60 within zones in 2015 ($p=0.085$). The only nominally significant pooled result, the 2021 area-only control (0.56, $p=0.035$), also does not survive correction (Holm 0.56). Pooled values below 0.5 are consistent with between-zone score offsets that work against prevalence differences; within-zone AUC removes that component. In cross-event transfer the area control does best (2015$\to$2021: 0.63; indicator models 0.56--0.60). Report intensity correlates weakly with all indicators ($|\rho|\le0.23$), and the SUS-1 mean percentile is unrelated to 2021 intensity ($\rho=0.00$).

\section{Discussion}
The administrative lists are genuine evidence that flooding was reported in a ward. As training labels, however, they are dominated by reporting opportunity: larger wards contain more streets that can be listed. They also have year-level timing and no negatives. Fitting a classifier to them would reproduce reporting patterns and could yield high random-split scores that collapse under spatial blocking \cite{roberts2017cv}.

The 2015 within-zone signal (AUC about 0.6, not significant after correction) may hint that terrain carries some information about where reports cluster within a zone. With 13 scored zones of about 13 wards each, the test has little power, and an area control of similar size argues for caution.

The radar result points in the same direction. Bright-VV diagnostics carry no detectable association with reported locations, consistent with the difficulty of urban SAR flood detection without coherence or building-aware methods \cite{mason2018urban,chini2019coherence}.

The structural finding has practical value regardless. In Chennai, terrain susceptibility peaks in peripheral, lower-density wards. Exposure and partial vulnerability peak in the core. Any combined index therefore depends strongly on weighting. We report the dimensions separately and flag five wards that are high on reported density, exposure and vulnerability only as an exploratory profile.

\emph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The study area is provisional.
\item GLO-30 is a surface model, and HAND is a Euclidean proxy.
\item The two population sources differ by a factor of about 3.
\item Social-sensitivity indicators are missing, and the partial vulnerability composite is sensitive to indicator choice (leave-one-out $\rho$ 0.56--0.85).
\item No E1 extent exists for any radar date.
\end{itemize}
The global permutation null ignores spatial autocorrelation. The zone-stratified null controls zone-level prevalence but not finer-scale autocorrelation, so even nominal $p$-values are, if anything, lenient. No calibration is attempted: without true negatives, presence-background scores cannot be calibrated to probabilities.

\section{Conclusion}
With the flood evidence that can presently be verified for Chennai, a multi-source framework can describe susceptibility, exposure, partial vulnerability and the consistency of independent reports. It cannot learn or validate a flood predictor. Supervised modelling becomes defensible only with at least one of:
\begin{itemize}
\item mapped extents within a few days of a radar pass;
\item dated reports;
\item credible negatives;
\item an independent second event for testing.
\end{itemize}
The released pipeline, dashboard and data contract make this boundary explicit and testable.
